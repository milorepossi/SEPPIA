Predicting peptide-HLA complex stability with a multilayer perceptron

Technical report · Rasmussen et al. dataset · 3 October 2026

A fully connected network regresses the logarithm of complex half-life from the stacked peptide and HLA pseudosequence. Trained independently on five constrained splits, it reaches a test RMSE of 1.17 ± 0.05 log units and a Spearman correlation of 0.770 ± 0.021, against 1.83 for a train-mean baseline. Error grows from 1.00 on test rows whose peptide and HLA both appear in training to 1.64 on rows carrying an HLA the model has never seen. The log offset ε was chosen by experiment (section 9): at 0.1 it improves rank correlation on all five splits relative to the 0.001 used initially.

1. Data and splits

The source is DATA/rasmussen_et_al_dataset.xlsx, 28,166 measured peptide-HLA complexes with columns allele, peptide (9 residues), thalf_hours, hla_seq (182 residues) and hla_pseudoseq (34 residues). It contains 5,002-8,751 distinct peptides depending on the split and only 74 distinct HLA pseudosequences.

Five train/test splits were produced beforehand by split_dataset.py and stored as NumPy archives. Each split is an independent 70/30 partition of all rows, constrained so that (i) at least 5 HLA sequences and 100 peptides occur only in the test set, and (ii) the train and test means of ln(x) and ln(x)² differ by at most 0.05 and 0.10. All five splits met both moment constraints on their first seed. They are not cross-validation folds: the holdouts are drawn independently, so test sets overlap between splits.

The validation set was not part of the original splits; it is carved out of each training set inside the training script, by a seeded random permutation of rows (--validation-fraction 0.1). Its only use is to decide when to stop and which weights to keep. The test sets are read once, after training ends.

The validation draw is random over rows, so validation peptides and HLAs also occur in training. Validation loss is therefore easier than test loss and is not a generalization estimate — only the test figures in section 7 are.

2. Feature encoding

Each example is the peptide stacked with the HLA pseudosequence, one-hot encoded:

  • Peptide: 9 positions, every peptide in the dataset is a 9-mer.
  • HLA: the 34-residue pseudosequence, not the full 182-residue chain. The pseudosequence keeps the residues lining the binding groove, so every input position is informative. With only 74 distinct HLA sequences in the data, the longer form would let the network treat the HLA block as an allele identifier and memorize it, which is precisely what fails on unseen alleles.
  • Alphabet: the 20 standard amino acids (ACDEFGHIKLMNPQRSTVWY); an unrecognised residue raises an error rather than being silently encoded as zeros.

This gives (9 + 34) × 20 = 860 binary input features. The encoding carries no notion of residue similarity: leucine and isoleucine are as unrelated as leucine and aspartate. A BLOSUM62 encoding, which would supply that similarity at the same input width, is the natural next experiment (section 10).

3. Target transform and zero handling

Half-lives span 0 to 256.7 hours, are strongly right-skewed (median 0.3 h among the non-zero values), and 5,679 rows — 20% of the dataset — are exactly zero. Regressing the raw value under a squared-error loss would let a handful of long-lived complexes dominate the gradient.

The model therefore predicts

y = ln(thalf_hours + ε),   ε = 0.1

Adding ε inside the logarithm is the regularization of the singularity at zero: it is smooth and monotone over the whole range, unlike clamping, and it keeps the 20% of zero rows at a single finite value, ln(0.1) ≈ −2.30. It is exposed as --epsilon.

The value 0.1 h is the reporting resolution of the assay — half-lives are given to one decimal — so it encodes the statement that the data cannot distinguish a zero from any value below the detection limit. Section 9 reports the experiment behind the choice: an earlier draft of this work used ε = 0.001, which placed the zero rows 4.6 log units below a typical 0.1 h measurement and let them dominate the loss.

Targets are then standardized with the training mean and standard deviation of each split, which conditions the optimization; predictions are mapped back before scoring, so every error in this report is in natural-log units of hours.

ε sets how far the zero spike sits below the rest of the distribution, so it rescales every error in this report. Absolute errors are therefore comparable only between runs sharing the same ε, and not with published figures that use a different transform.

4. Architecture

A fully connected feed-forward network, ReLU activations, dropout after each hidden layer, single linear output. Three weight layers in total: two hidden, one output.

5. Training and regularization strategy

Four regularizing mechanisms act together; the first is about the target, the other three about the weights.

Target ε
0.1 added before the logarithm (section 3), removing the singularity at zero without discarding the 20% of zero-stability rows.
Dropout
p = 0.2 after each hidden layer, active in training only.
Weight decay
1×10−5, applied by Adam to all parameters.
Early stopping
Validation MSE is evaluated every epoch; the weights of the best epoch are restored at the end. Training halts after 25 epochs without improvement, or at 300 epochs.

6. Convergence

Figure 1 shows, per split, the training and validation MSE in log units and the mean gradient L2 norm per epoch on a logarithmic axis. All five runs converge in the same way: the gradient norm rises over the first ~10 epochs as the weights leave their initialization, then decays monotonically; training loss flattens; validation loss reaches a plateau near 1.0-1.2 and then drifts upward as the network begins to overfit, which is where early stopping intervenes.

Two results stand out. Generalization to unseen peptides costs 32% RMSE: with thousands of distinct 9-mers in training, the network learns position-wise peptide chemistry that transfers, if imperfectly. Generalization to unseen HLAs costs 64% and reaches 1.80 in the worst split, approaching the 1.83 of a model that ignores the sequences entirely. Performance on seen combinations is by contrast remarkably stable, RMSE 1.001 ± 0.012 across splits, so nearly all the split-to-split variation in the headline number comes from which alleles were withheld.

The unseen-HLA figure should be read as a weak estimate, not a stable benchmark: 5 held-out alleles per split, with overlapping test sets between splits, means the SD of 0.59 understates the true uncertainty across possible allele choices.

8. Structure of the error

Figure 3 plots every test prediction against its measurement, pooled over the five splits. The vertical stripes at the left are the discreteness of the assay: half-lives are reported to one decimal, so the measured axis has few distinct values there, the leftmost being the zero rows at ln(0.1).

The cloud follows the diagonal but is visibly flatter than it, which is the signature of regression to the mean under a squared-error loss: the model compresses its predictions toward the centre of the distribution. The median prediction per decile sits above equality at the low end and below it at the high end, crossing near the middle of the range.

Read as a stability call

Thresholding the regression turns signed error into false positives (predicted stable, measured unstable) and false negatives. Which error dominates depends entirely on where the threshold is placed.

At a 1-hour threshold the two error types are balanced. Below it false positives dominate, as the over-predicted zero and near-zero rows cross the line; above it false negatives dominate, as shrinkage pulls genuinely stable complexes below it. Screening for strongly stable complexes (> 2 h) therefore loses about a quarter of them, which is the practical cost of the shrinkage and the main target for future work.

9. Choosing the log offset ε

ε is not a free parameter: it decides how far the 20% of zero-stability rows sit below the measurable ones, and therefore how much of the loss they absorb. Four values were trained on all five splits with every other setting fixed.

Comparison is not straightforward, because ε changes the units of the target and so rescales RMSE directly — raw RMSE falls from 2.42 to 0.64 across this sweep almost entirely by rescaling. Two transform-independent measures are used instead: Spearman correlation, invariant under any monotone transform, and an RMSE computed after mapping every model's predictions back to hours and into one fixed space, ln(t + 0.1).

The splits are paired, so per-split differences carry more information than the standard deviations. Against ε = 0.001, both 0.1 and 1.0 improve Spearman on all five splits (+0.028 on average), improve common-space RMSE on all five (−0.13), and reduce the > 2 h false-negative rate on all five (−3.4 and −4.0 points). The mechanism is the one Table 7 makes explicit: with a larger ε the zero rows stop monopolising the loss and the network spends its capacity on the measurable range.

Between 0.1 and 1.0 there is nothing to choose statistically — the differences are well inside the split-to-split spread. 0.1 was adopted on two grounds beyond the metrics:

  • It matches the assay. Half-lives are reported to one decimal, so 0.1 h is the resolution and plausibly the detection limit. ε = 0.1 states that zero and 0.05 h are indistinguishable, which is true of the data.
  • ε = 1 flattens the low range. Since ln(1 + t) ≈ t for small t, the target becomes effectively linear in hours for the 58% of rows below 1 h, discarding the log resolution that separates 0.1 from 0.5 h. Its median prediction for a truly-zero complex is 0.19 h, nearly twice the assay resolution.
Caveat: the architecture and learning rate were tuned for none of these settings, so some of the gap might close under retuning. More fundamentally, tuning ε is a workaround for a censoring problem — if zeros mean "below detection", the principled treatment is a censored likelihood that reads them as t < 0.1 rather than t = 0.1.

10. Limitations and next steps

  1. Censoring is handled by a constant, not a model. ε = 0.1 is an informed choice (section 9) but still asserts a value for measurements that have none. A censored likelihood, or the bounded transform s = 2−t0/t used by NetMHCstabpan, would treat the detection limit explicitly and make results comparable with the literature.
  2. Predictions are shrunk toward the mean. The ±0.85 log-unit bias at the extremes (Table 7, Figure 3) costs about a quarter of strongly stable complexes at a 2-hour threshold. If a specific operating threshold matters, a classifier at that threshold, or a two-stage binder-then-stability model, would likely beat thresholding this regressor.
  3. Validation is optimistic. Holding out whole peptides and HLAs for validation, mirroring how the test sets are built, would stop training at a point tuned for unseen sequences; this most plausibly affects the unseen-HLA result.
  4. One-hot ignores residue similarity. BLOSUM62 encoding is a drop-in replacement at the same input width and is the most likely single improvement for unseen HLAs.
  5. No hyperparameter search. Widths, dropout, learning rate and weight decay are untuned defaults. Any search must use the validation sets only.
  6. Few HLAs. With 74 pseudosequences, allele-level generalization cannot be estimated precisely from 5 held-out alleles per split; leave-one-allele-out over all 74 would measure it properly, at 74 training runs.
  7. Date-recovered targets. 11,907 of 28,166 half-lives (42%) were stored as dates in the source workbook and recovered as day.month by the splitting script. The recovery is consistent with one-decimal values, but it is lossy in principle and worth confirming against the original publication.
